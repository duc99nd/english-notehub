# Can and Can't

## What "can" does

**Can** is one of the most useful English verbs because it does three jobs at once:

1. **Ability** — "I can swim." (= I am able to swim.)
2. **Permission** — "Can I sit here?" (= Is it OK for me to sit here?)
3. **Polite request** — "Can you help me?" (= Would you help me?)

The negative **can't** works for all three:

- "I can't swim." (= I'm not able to.)
- "You can't park here." (= You're not allowed to.)
- "Can't you see I'm busy?" (= Please notice / pay attention.)

> **Pattern**: `Subject + can + base verb`. Notice — no "to", no "-ing", just the base form. "I can **to** swim" and "I can **swimming**" are both wrong.

## Three forms

| Use      | Pattern            | Example       |
| -------- | ------------------ | ------------- |
| Positive | `S + can + verb`   | I can swim.   |
| Negative | `S + can't + verb` | I can't swim. |
| Question | `Can + S + verb?`  | Can you swim? |

> **Note**: There is **no "s"** on "can", even with he/she/it. "She **cans** swim" is wrong. "Can" is the same for every subject.

## Ability — talking about skills

> **Pattern**: `Subject + can / can't + verb` (for skills you have or don't have right now)

| English                  | Vietnamese                        |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| I can speak English.     | Tôi có thể nói tiếng Anh.         |
| She can play the piano.  | Cô ấy có thể chơi piano.          |
| He can't drive a car.    | Anh ấy không biết lái xe.         |
| They can cook very well. | Họ nấu ăn rất ngon.               |
| We can't come today.     | Hôm nay chúng tôi không đến được. |

Two important points:

- **No "to"** between can and the verb. ❌ "I can to swim" → ✅ "I can swim."
- **No "-ing"** after can. ❌ "I can swimming" → ✅ "I can swim."

## Permission — asking if something is OK

> **Pattern**: `Can I + verb?` (= Is it OK for me to ...?)

| English                  | Use                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Can I sit here?          | Asking about a seat           |
| Can I open the window?   | Asking before doing something |
| Can I use your phone?    | Asking to borrow              |
| Can I leave early today? | Asking your boss              |

Soft alternatives (more polite):

- "**May** I sit here?" (very formal)
- "**Could** I sit here?" (slightly more polite than "can")
- "**Is it OK if** I sit here?" (informal)

## Polite requests

> **Pattern**: `Can you + verb?` (= Would you ... please?)

| English                 | Use                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Can you help me?        | Asking for help        |
| Can you pass the salt?  | At the dinner table    |
| Can you say that again? | You didn't hear        |
| Can you wait a moment?  | Asking someone to wait |

> **Tip**: Add "**please**" or "**excuse me**" to make requests softer. "Can you help me, **please**?" sounds much better than just "Can you help me?"

## Short answers

| Question       | Yes            | No              |
| -------------- | -------------- | --------------- |
| Can you swim?  | Yes, I can.    | No, I can't.    |
| Can she cook?  | Yes, she can.  | No, she can't.  |
| Can he drive?  | Yes, he can.   | No, he can't.   |
| Can they come? | Yes, they can. | No, they can't. |

> **Pattern**: Don't say "**Yes, I can't**" — that's a contradiction. The negative answer is just "No, I can't."

## Listen to a real conversation

> **Anna**: Hi, Linh. Can you swim?
>
> **Linh**: Yes, I can. I can swim very well. Can you cook?
>
> **Anna**: No, I can't. But I can order food very well.
>
> **Linh**: That's funny. Can I ask you something?
>
> **Anna**: Sure. What?
>
> **Linh**: Can you help me with my English homework? I can't understand this question.
>
> **Anna**: Of course. Can you show me the question?
>
> **Linh**: It's right here. "Can you speak three languages?"
>
> **Anna**: Hmm. I can speak two — English and Vietnamese. I can't speak three. What about you?
>
> **Linh**: I can speak Vietnamese and a little English. Only two.

Notice the rhythm: every "can / can't" answers the question directly. There are no long sentences — short, clear, conversational.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ "I can to swim." — no "to" after can. ✅ "I can swim."
- ❌ "She cans swim." — no "s" on can. ✅ "She can swim."
- ❌ "I don't can swim." — don't + can doesn't work. ✅ "I can't swim."
- ❌ "Can you to help me?" — no "to" in questions either. ✅ "Can you help me?"
- ❌ "Yes, I can't." — contradiction. ✅ "No, I can't."

## Culture note: "Can I" vs "May I"

Grammatically, **"can" and "may"** are interchangeable for permission. In real life:

- "**Can** I use your phone?" — casual, friendly
- "**May** I use your phone?" — formal, polite

Use "may" with teachers, older people, and in business. Use "can" with friends and peers. Most native speakers actually use "can" everywhere — but knowing "may" makes you sound more refined.

## Scenario practice

### Situation

A small group is dividing tasks for an event. Say two things you can do, one thing you cannot do, and ask for help.

> **Training note:** This situation and its details are fictional. They are designed for language practice, not presented as a real event, policy, study, or legal interpretation.

### Controlled practice

Take two example sentences from the lesson. Transform the first with the target structure, then revise the second to change emphasis or level of certainty without changing the core facts.

### Guided task

Write 20–70 words for the situation. Use the target structure at least twice, underline those choices, and add one sentence explaining how one structure changes emphasis, time, condition, or certainty.

### Model response

> **Draft:** I can swim very well. Can you swim? She can't speak French, but she can speak English. Excuse me, can I sit here?
>
> **Language choice:** I keep the message short, use familiar words, and finish with one clear next step. Other well-supported responses are possible.

### Why the model works

The model shows one defensible route through the task; it is not a script to memorize. Compare its task completion, language accuracy, audience fit, and clarity with your response, then revise once.
