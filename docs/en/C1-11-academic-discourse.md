# Academic Discourse

## Academic vocabulary

Academic writing at C1 level requires precise vocabulary and appropriate hedging.

### Core academic terms

| Term             | Definition           | Example                                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **paradigm**     | Model or framework   | "A new paradigm in economics."           |
| **methodology**  | Approach to research | "The methodology is robust."             |
| **empirical**    | Based on observation | "Empirical evidence supports the claim." |
| **literature**   | Existing research    | "The literature confirms this."          |
| **findings**     | Research results     | "The findings are significant."          |
| **implications** | Consequences         | "The implications are far-reaching."     |

## Hedging: claiming with caution

Academic writers use **hedges** to qualify their claims and show appropriate caution.

> **Pattern**: `The findings [hedge verb] that...`

### Hedge verbs

| Strength | Verb                          |
| -------- | ----------------------------- |
| Strong   | demonstrate, establish, prove |
| Medium   | suggest, indicate, imply      |
| Weak     | appear, seem, tend            |

## Hedging adjectives and adverbs

| Hedge       | Example                                         |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| arguably    | "This is arguably the most significant change." |
| purportedly | "The purportedly leaked document..."            |
| tentatively | "We tentatively conclude that..."               |
| broadly     | "Broadly speaking, the trend continues."        |

## Reporting verbs in literature reviews

| Function     | Verb                          |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Agreement    | confirm, corroborate, support |
| Disagreement | contradict, challenge, refute |
| Neutral      | note, observe, mention        |

## Exercise: Replace with academic equivalents

1. "The data shows..." → "The data \_\_\_..."
2. "This proves X." → "This \_\_\_ X." (less strong)
3. "Many researchers think..." → "The \_\_\_ confirms..."

## Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson, you can complete a four-turn exchange about academic discourse, choosing expressions that fit the relationship and immediate goal.

## Scenario practice

### Situation

Discuss a fictional workplace study whose findings show correlation but not causation. Use cautious research language and identify a gap.

> **Training note:** This situation and its details are fictional. They are designed for language practice, not presented as a real event, policy, study, or legal interpretation.

### Controlled practice

Choose three expressions from **Academic Discourse**. Label each one as an opener, key detail, clarification, or next step. Remove any expression that does not fit the relationship in the situation.

### Guided task

Write a four-to-six-turn exchange for this situation. Use at least three target expressions, respond to the other speaker's information, and end with a question, confirmation, or agreed action.

### Model response

> **Partner:** We need to respond to the situation above. What would you say first?
>
> **You:** The findings suggest a correlation between the variables.
>
> **Partner:** What detail should we confirm before we act?
>
> **You:** The methodology warrants further investigation. The literature indicates a gap in current research.

### Why the model works

The model shows one defensible route through the task; it is not a script to memorize. Compare its task completion, language accuracy, audience fit, and clarity with your response, then revise once.

### Transfer task

Explain the finding to a non-academic operations leader. Keep the target language, but change the details and register needed for the new context.
