# Food & Drinks

## Why food vocabulary first

Every meal is a vocabulary test. In one restaurant visit you say "I'd like", "Can I have", "some", "a cup of", "the bill please". Master these and you can eat anywhere in the English-speaking world without pointing at the menu.

## Three food categories

| Category    | Vietnamese         | Rule                                            | Examples                                                           |
| ----------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Countable   | thứ đếm được       | You can use "a / an" and plural                 | apple, egg, chicken, sandwich                                      |
| Uncountable | thứ không đếm được | No plural, no "a/an", needs "some" / "a cup of" | rice, water, milk, bread, meat                                     |
| Both        | dùng cả hai        | Depends on meaning                              | coffee (drink vs. portion), fruit (uncountable vs. specific types) |

> **Pattern**: "a/an" goes with **singular countable**: an apple, an egg. **Uncountable** nouns need "**some**" or a **measure word**: some rice, a cup of coffee, two glasses of water.

## Common drinks

| English     | Vietnamese | Count?               | Container                |
| ----------- | ---------- | -------------------- | ------------------------ |
| water       | nước       | uncountable          | a glass of / a bottle of |
| coffee      | cà phê     | uncountable          | a cup of / a mug of      |
| tea         | trà        | uncountable          | a cup of / a pot of      |
| milk        | sữa        | uncountable          | a glass of               |
| juice       | nước ép    | uncountable          | a glass of               |
| soda / coke | nước ngọt  | count or uncountable | a can of / a glass of    |
| beer        | bia        | count or uncountable | a bottle of / a glass of |

> **Pattern for containers**: `a cup of coffee`, `a glass of water`, `a bottle of wine`, `a can of soda`. The noun after "of" is **always singular**, even for multiple cups: "two cups of coffee" — not "two coffees" in formal English, though "two coffees" is fine in casual speech.

## Countable vs uncountable — the four key words

| Word         | Use with uncountable      | Use with countable                |
| ------------ | ------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| some / any   | I have **some** rice. (✓) | I have **some** apples. (✓ also)  |
| a lot of     | a lot of rice             | a lot of apples                   |
| much / many  | much rice (✓)             | **many** apples (✓) — not "much"  |
| little / few | little rice (✓)           | **few** apples (✓) — not "little" |

> **Note**: "**Some**" works with both. The classic mistake is using "many" with uncountable: ❌ "I have many water." ✅ "I have much water." (or better: "I have a lot of water.")

## Ordering at a restaurant — the script

> **Pattern A — Polite order**: `I would like [some] [food], please.`
>
> **Pattern B — Question**: `Could I have [the menu / the bill]?`
>
> **Pattern C — Ask for recommendation**: `What do you recommend?`

### Step-by-step ordering

| Step                 | What to say                                              |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Sit down          | (Nothing — wait to be seated)                            |
| 2. Get menu          | "Could I see the menu, please?"                          |
| 3. Ask about dish    | "What do you recommend?" or "What's in the [dish]?"      |
| 4. Order             | "I'd like the chicken, please." or "I'll have the fish." |
| 5. Order drinks      | "A glass of water, please."                              |
| 6. Ask for the check | "Could I have the bill, please?"                         |
| 7. Pay + tip         | (Tip 15-20% in the US; not customary in many countries)  |

> **Tip**: "**I'd like**" is short for "I would like". Both are correct, but "I'd like" is faster and friendlier. "**I'll have**" is even more casual — like talking to a friend at a café.

## Listen to a real restaurant order

> **Waiter**: Good evening. Could I see the menu, please?
>
> **Anna**: Sure, here you go. Are you ready to order, or do you need a minute?
>
> **Waiter**: I need a minute, please.
>
> **Anna**: Of course. I'll come back.
>
> _(a minute later)_
>
> **Waiter**: I'm ready. I'd like the chicken with rice, please.
>
> **Anna**: Sure. Would you like a drink?
>
> **Waiter**: Yes. A glass of water, please.
>
> **Anna**: Anything else?
>
> **Waiter**: What do you recommend for dessert?
>
> **Anna**: The chocolate cake is very popular. Or the fruit salad if you want something lighter.
>
> **Waiter**: I'll have the chocolate cake.
>
> **Anna**: Great choice. I'll be right back with your water.

Notice how the waiter uses **I'd like**, **a glass of**, and **What do you recommend** — the three patterns from this lesson.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ "I would like **a** water, please." — water is uncountable. ✅ "I would like **a glass of** water, please."
- ❌ "I want rice, please." — too direct, sounds rude. ✅ "I would like **some** rice, please."
- ❌ "Two **coffees**, please." — technically OK casual, but "two cups of coffee" is standard. ✅ "Two cups of coffee, please."
- ❌ "I'd like **a** chicken." — chicken (as food) is uncountable. ✅ "I'd like **some** chicken, please." or "I'd like **the chicken dish**."
- ❌ "I want the **bills**." — bill is singular. ✅ "**The bill**, please." (uncountable concept)

## Your turn: order a meal

Imagine you're at a café. Write your order using **"I'd like..."** and **measure words**.

> Waiter: Are you ready to order?
>
> You: Yes. I'd like ****\_\_**** for my main, and ****\_\_**** to drink.
>
> Waiter: Anything else?
>
> You: ****\_\_****, please. (a dessert / side / extra)
>
> Waiter: Sure. I'll be right back.

Then write what the waiter brings:

> The waiter brings: ****\_\_**** and ****\_\_****.

- [ ] I used "I'd like" or "I'll have"

- [ ] I said "the bill, please" not "the bills"

## Culture note: tipping and café culture

**Tipping in the US**: 15-20% of the bill is standard. Not tipping is considered rude. In the UK, service charge is often included. In Vietnam, no tipping culture.

**Drinking water**: Tap water is safe in the US/UK/Australia but not in Vietnam or much of SE Asia. In restaurants, just say "sparkling or still?" if the waiter asks — sparkling water has bubbles, still water does not.

**"I'd like the chicken"**: At many cafés, "the chicken" refers to a specific named dish on the menu (e.g., "Grilled Lemon Chicken"). If you're not sure what's on the menu, ask: "What is the chicken?"
